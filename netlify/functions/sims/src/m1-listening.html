Reading support for blind people

Janice: I'm Janice Clark, and I'm the manager of the charity called The Hub, which provides a wide range of volunteer services for blind people, such as providing recordings of books and magazines. The Hub charity is based in a small village called Ivy Brook, which it's called home since 1990. We now have nearly 75 volunteers working for us in some capacity, but when it was first set up in 198011, there were only 25. All of them were doing recorded readings for the blind.

Janice: Nowadays, we offer a great deal more, which means we need a wide range of volunteers with different expertise, as I'll explain in a few moments.

Janice: The Hub is entirely dependent on donations. We have very generous gifts from local businesses, though the most significant contribution has always come from individual supporters12. We also get an increasing amount from school groups who arrange various benefit events to make money for us.

Janice: Some people hear about us because a friend or relative with sight problems already makes use of our services and tells them about how valuable The Hub is, or they may hear about us from someone they meet who is already a volunteer. That's how most people join us13, in fact. But our website also attracted quite a lot of interest from people who want to support us.

Janice: There are certain areas of the operation, where we particularly need volunteers at the moment. This week, one group are involved in addressing envelopes containing recordings in digital form, which have been requested by almost 10,000 users all over the country.

Janice: But what we're specifically looking for at this time are people to help make sure our lists of recordings and other materials are accurate14, so we don't waste time on unnecessary repeats.

Janice: I feel strongly that we're a friendly and open organization, and we listen carefully to any comments about negative aspects of working at The Hub. Ivy Brook is an interesting location. It's very close to the countryside, but there are some local shops to nip out to and buy stuff for lunch or a snack. There's plenty of space. In our old town center location, we had lots of problems with parking, but not here.

Janice: However, the volunteers tell us the rooms can feel rather cold in the winter15, so we're investing in a new boiler. Until it's installed, you'd better wrap up.

Janice: We're also a dynamic organization, always on the lookout to provide services our users really need. Preparing an Environmental Report

Mark James: My name's Mark James, and I'm an officer with the Environmental Protection Agency. Our job is to examine building proposals sent to the government by construction companies to determine their risk to the environment. We do this in the form of an environmental report, which I'll now outline for you today.

Mark James: Firstly, the company sends us their project description, outlining each part of their proposal.

Mark James: In the next stage, we produce an area description. We need to formally catalog the wildlife within the project's proposed perimeter and determine whether any species will be threatened by the development.

Mark James: Next is the mitigation stage, where we use the information from stage two to make recommendations for how the company can avoid any negative environmental impact. With the construction of wind turbines, for example, we might recommend avoiding the months when birds are nesting31.

Mark James: Next, we produce the non-technical summary, which looks at the operation's objectives and its potential impact on the area in question. We make sure the information doesn't contain any jargon that will interfere with the message, and that it's written in simple language. We then issue this to the public32, and invite feedback.

Mark James: And finally, we prepare the final report, communicating our proposed action to the construction company.

Mark James: Let's look at one particular construction proposal that we worked on recently for a project on Barrow Island. Barrow Island is around 70 kilometers off the western Australian coast. It's unique because thousands of years ago it was separated from the mainland.

Mark James: The flora and fauna of the island are different because they've been able to grow unaffected by the events and factors that have, over time, influenced the natural environment of the mainland. By this, I mean the introduction of non-native animals like foxes and cats. The island has also been spared the environmental impact of farming33.

Mark James: I should say at the outset that industry has already existed on the island for several decades. The first was in the 1930s when fishing34 was established here on a commercial basis, about 10 men would inhabit the island for six months of the year and operate within a 20-kilometer radius during the annual season.

Mark James: Then, in the 1950s, mining35 was established, employing around 150 people.

Mark James: The existing industry I've just described is a completely different issue to the construction project proposed by Chandler Developers, though. Chandler Developers wanted to construct a plant on Barrow Island that would allow them to extract gas36 from way out in the deep waters off the coast of Australia. They would then clean it up and compress it into a liquid37, and then sell it to a range of places like China and Japan and so forth.

Mark James: Construction was to occur over three years, employing around 6000 people, bringing in millions of tons of equipment and materials. Now, of course, with this kind of major invasion, the government was deeply concerned about the environmental impact.

Mark James: Barrow Island's two main beaches, which are home to a variety of marine life, are located on either side of the proposed development. The amount of noise38 generated by this project would cause extreme anxiety to the local turtle population in particular, as they use these beaches for breeding and laying eggs,

Mark James: We were also concerned that any baby turtles that hatch and do manage to find the water could become disorientated and totally at the mercy of predators. Fortunately, saltwater crocodiles are not found in this area, but sharks39 are common and represent the main risk to the local marine life, and particularly the defenseless baby turtles.

Mark James: Another issue we had is with the sheer size of the project, which would require large numbers of people to come onto the island during the construction period. This would create a very real danger of pests being brought with them. It would be catastrophic for the wildlife, and indeed the entire ecology of the island, if, for example, a population of rats40 were to become established.

Mark James: So, there were very real issues with this project, and our report recommended that it not proceed.
